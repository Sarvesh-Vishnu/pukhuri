01 — A blue planet, mostly out of reach

We call it the blue planet. We're describing water we can't drink.

Picture all the water on Earth as a hundred drops.

Saltwater & ice — 97.5% Freshwater — 2.5% Liquid, surface, reachable — ~0.5%

Of every hundred drops, fewer than three are fresh. Most of those are frozen in ice or buried too deep to lift. Barely one drop — the rivers, lakes and shallow groundwater that almost everyone alive actually uses — carries the entire human enterprise.JMP·USGS

Scarcity isn't the surprising part. The surprising part is how good we've gotten at writing as if that one drop were evenly poured — one promise per person, signed and filed. They are not. Where you are born decides how many drops reach you, and whether they're safe when they arrive.

02 — The idea this whole thing rests on

On paper, everyone already has water.

In 2010 the UN recognised water as a human right. Most nations enshrine it. India is wiring a tap into every rural home. The plans are real, ambitious, and beautifully written. The gap between them and a glass you'd let a child drink is the subject of everything that follows.

What the page says

The right to water

  • § A safe, sufficient, accessible supply for every household.
  • § 55 litres per person per day, piped to the home.
  • § Tested water, published quality, a number to call.
  • § Plans, budgets, dashboards, deadlines — all filed.
What the tap does

The reach of it

  • → A connection that runs an hour every third day.
  • → Clear water that quietly carries arsenic or fluoride.
  • → A test that was never done, or never shared.
  • → A pipe that was laid beside a sewer.

The work isn't to mock the paper.
It's to close the distance between the paper and the pipe.

03 — The line nobody draws on a map

Two billion people live on the wrong side of a promise.

Not in one region — everywhere the paper outran the plumbing.

Each bar is people, not percentages of a budget. Safely managed = water on premises, available when needed, free of contamination.JMP 2023

Ten countries on the wrong side of the line.

A deliberate spread — Middle-Eastern aquifers run dry, Asian giants outgrow their rivers, and across Sub-Saharan Africa the barrier is distance, not scarcity. India sits in company, not isolation. Choose a country.

The burden isn't shared evenly even within a household. Where water has to be fetched, it is overwhelmingly women and girls who walk for it — hours a day that don't appear in any plan, subtracted from school and work and rest.

The global numbers are the wide shot. To see how the gap actually behaves — how a country can pour billions into pipes and still bury people who drank from them — we go to the place with the most people, the least margin, and the most water written down. India.

05 — When the pipe betrays you

Indore is India's cleanest city. In December, its water killed.

For eight years running, Indore won the title of cleanest city in India.Swachh In late December 2025, residents of Bhagirathpura noticed their tap water had turned bitter and foul. With no real alternative, many kept drinking it.

0 people dead — including a six-month-old infant
0 fell ill across one locality
0 residents screened, door to door, after the fact

The cause was almost banal: a public toilet had been built directly over a drinking-water pipeline, with no septic tank. Sewage seeped into the supply.Indore 2025 The water tested positive for bacteria found in human waste.

A city can win every award for cleanliness and still lay a pipe beside a sewer.

That is the whole thesis in one street. The plan said safe water. The audit said cleanest city. The reality was sewage, and a funeral for an infant. Indore is not an outlier — it's the visible version of what 30 million illnesses a year look like when one of them makes the news.

06 — From reading to knowing

07 — The dilemma I'm writing inside

This site was built with AI. AI drinks water. So let's be honest about it.

Data centres use water. So do electric cars, beef, rice, almonds, ethanol, the phone in your hand. The guilt is real — but guilt is a bad accountant. Not all water use is equal, and pretending it is helps no one in Bhagirathpura.

The first honest distinction: withdrawn vs. consumed.

Withdrawn
0 bn L
borrowed from a source, much of it returned. One tech firm's own data centres, 2023.
Consumed / evaporated
0 bn L
gone to the sky as vapour — and ~80% of it was drinking-quality water.UCR

A 100-megawatt AI facility can consume around 2 million litres a day — the water of about 6,500 households — and most data centres evaporate roughly 80% of what they draw.IEA Globally, data centres consume an estimated 560 billion litres a year, heading toward 1,200 billion by 2030.

The second: a litre is not a litre.

What actually makes water use harmful isn't the headline number — it's four things underneath it.

Quality

Potable vs. recycled

Evaporating treated drinking water in a thirsty city is worlds apart from a closed loop on recycled or grey water.

Place

Where it's drawn

A litre in rain-soaked Kerala isn't a litre in arid Rajasthan. By the 2050s ~45% of data centres may sit in high water stress.S&P

Fate

Returned vs. gone

Withdrawn-and-returned water re-enters the system. Evaporated water leaves the local basin entirely.

Honesty

Measured vs. guessed

"One prompt = a bottle of water" is largely a journalistic shortcut. Real intensity varies up to 10,000× by site.LBNL

The third: scale and proportion.

Put your AI prompt next to the rest of an ordinary day. The bars are litres.

The model query is a rounding error beside a hamburger. That doesn't make data centres harmless — at city scale, in the wrong place, they bite. It means the biggest levers most of us hold are on the plate, not the keyboard.WFN

The answer to guilt isn't to stop.
It's to know which litres matter, and spend them where the rain is.

There is genuine progress: closed-loop, zero-evaporation cooling can cut a single facility's water by 125 million litres a year.2025 The fix for AI's thirst is the same as the fix for everything in this story — measure honestly, build in the right place, and don't pretend the paper is the pipe.

08 — Closing the gap

Make the paper truer. Then make it real.

Being grateful and educated is the floor, not the ceiling. The point of seeing the gap is to stand in it. A few honest places to start —

01

Know your own litres

Learn the water cost of what you eat, wear and buy — the plate moves far more water than the keyboard. Eat down the chart. Waste less of what's already treated.

02

Read the businesses around you

Ask where companies, bottlers and data centres draw their water, whether it's potable, and whether they're in a stressed basin. Demand it's disclosed, not guessed.

03

Test, report, harvest

Get water tested at an accredited lab before trusting a new source. Report foul supply early. Harvest rain where you can — in many states it's already the law on paper.

04

Fund the people closing the gap

Support charities and local bodies bringing safe water to those without it — the most direct way to turn someone else's paper promise into a real glass of water.

Water on paper is a promise.
Water in the glass is the proof.
